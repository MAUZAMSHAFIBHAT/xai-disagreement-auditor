When explanations disagree,
which one do you trust?

A per-prediction Explanation Trust Score for post-hoc XAI — and a ground-truth benchmark testing whether disagreement actually signals that explanations are wrong.
Mauzam Shafi Bhat · Duke University · Fall 2026 · Instructor: Dr. Brinnae Bent
r=.61
Correlation between how much five explanation methods agree and how often they identify the true drivers — across 400 predictions.

1 The problem

Post-hoc explainers are how high-stakes model decisions get justified — a denied loan, a flagged tumour — and increasingly how organisations demonstrate compliance. But run several on the same prediction and they routinely disagree: different top features, different rankings, sometimes opposite directions. Krishna et al. (2022) named this the disagreement problem and found practitioners resolve it by intuition or by defaulting to a favourite tool.

Nobody is told when an explanation is unreliable. A single SHAP plot looks equally authoritative whether every method agrees with it or none do.

Research questions

  • Q1 Can disagreement be quantified into a usable per-prediction reliability signal?
  • Q2 When methods disagree, are explanations actually wrong more often?
  • Q3 What drives disagreement — the data, or the model?

The five methods audited

Shapley (sampling) Game-theoretic credit over random feature orderings
LIME Local weighted linear surrogate
Integrated Gradients Path integral from an average input to this one
SmoothGrad × Input Noise-averaged local gradient × input
Occlusion Prediction drop when a feature is marginalised out

All five implemented from scratch in NumPy so they share one model, one background sample and one baseline — disagreement is methodological, not an artefact of library defaults. Verified exact on linear models; Shapley matches the reference shap library at r > 0.99.

Verified two independent ways

  • Faithfulness — PGI / PGU / deletion curves against a random-attribution control: does the explanation match the model?
  • Ground truth — synthetic data whose true drivers are known by construction: does it match reality? 8 features, 4 informative, ρ swept 0 → 0.95, 4 model families, 20 predictions each.

4 Finding 1 · Disagreement predicts error

The score earns its keep. It is computed without any ground truth, yet tracks ground-truth correctness at r = +0.61 — so an analyst can know when to distrust an explanation on real data, where truth is unavailable.

5 Finding 2 · Correlation erodes agreement

Interpretability is a property of the model, not only of the tool. Tree ensembles and the neural net lose ≈ 10 points of agreement and ≈ 17 points of explanation accuracy. Logistic regression is unmoved (≈ 94) at every correlation level.

6 Finding 3 · The method matters

Local gradients fail on trees; beware circular evaluation. Tree ensembles are piecewise constant, so SmoothGrad's local gradient is near-zero and uninformative. Separately, Occlusion wins the removal-based faithfulness tests — but those tests use Occlusion's own removal operation. Against ground truth it is mid-table.

7 Same accuracy, different explainability

A model-selection argument. Accuracy cannot tell you how explainable a model will be. Where explanations carry legal or clinical weight, this makes the trade-off visible before deployment rather than after a contested decision.

8 Limitations & misuse risks

  • Agreement is not truth. All five can agree and still be wrong — notably when the model genuinely relies on a proxy. The score is a warning light, not a certificate.
  • Circularity. Removal-based faithfulness metrics favour removal-based explainers; reported openly inside the tool.
  • Off-manifold perturbation. Under strong correlation every perturbation method queries the model on inputs that could not occur — part of why they diverge.
  • Explanation-washing. The likeliest misuse is citing a high Trust Score as evidence that a model is fair or compliant. It certifies only that methods concur; it says nothing about accuracy, bias or appropriateness. It belongs alongside model validation, never instead of it.
  • Score-shopping. k, the background sample or the method set could be tuned until the number flatters. Mitigations: fixed defaults, seeded runs, exported reports that record the configuration.
  • Scope. Tabular binary classification; multi-class one-vs-rest. Bands (70 / 50) are reasoned, not yet calibrated.

9 Who it is for, and what is next

  • Data scientists gain a reason to choose a method rather than a habit.
  • Auditors and compliance teams gain a per-decision flag for "do not present a single explanation here".
  • Affected people deserve a reason for a decision that does not depend on which library the analyst happened to import.

Future work

  • Calibrate the bands. Fit Trust Score → probability-of-correctness by isotonic regression on the benchmark, turning a 0–100 heuristic into a stated error rate.
  • On-manifold explanations. Conditional Shapley values, to test whether the correlation effect is an artefact of off-manifold sampling.
  • Stability as a sixth signal. Re-run each method under different seeds to separate sampling noise from genuine methodological disagreement.
  • Human study. Does showing a Trust Score change what practitioners do with an explanation? The problem was found through practitioner interviews; the remedy should be tested the same way.
  • Beyond tabular. Text and vision models, where attribution disagreement is less studied and arguably more consequential.
